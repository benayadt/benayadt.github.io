I have been a software engineer for the last 13 years, and I can confidently say that in the last year I have seen more changes in my day-to-day work than in the previous 11 years combined.

The reason is simple: AI.

That may sound like an exaggeration, but it really is not. For most of my career, change in software engineering felt gradual. New frameworks appeared, cloud adoption accelerated, teams refined their delivery practices, and tooling improved year after year. But the core of the work remained familiar. You still had to understand the problem, design a solution, write the code, debug it, test it, and maintain it over time.

Now the pace feels different. It feels compressed. Work that used to take hours can sometimes be done in minutes. Tasks that required a lot of manual effort can now start from a surprisingly good first draft. And because of that, the nature of the job is shifting.

## Programming is not the same as software engineering

Before AI became part of everyday developer workflows, a big part of software engineering was centered around programming itself.

I am deliberately saying *programming* and not *software engineering*, because the two are not exactly the same thing.

Programming is the act of writing code. Software engineering is broader. It is about solving a problem in a way that is reliable, maintainable, understandable, and useful in a real context. It is about understanding constraints, making trade-offs, thinking about change over time, and building software that your future self, or someone else on your team, can still work with months or years later.

That difference matters even more in the era of AI.

## What took time before AI

For a long time, becoming effective as a software engineer required spending years building a very specific kind of knowledge.

It takes time to learn how to program well. It takes time to understand how code really works. It takes time to debug efficiently, to reason about performance, to design software that is testable, and to write systems that can evolve without collapsing under their own complexity.

None of this is easy.

Early in my career, a lot of my effort went into exactly that. I spent a lot of time learning the mechanics of the craft: writing code, reading code, fixing bugs, understanding failure modes, and slowly developing intuition about what good software looks like. That investment is unavoidable. It is how you build the foundation.

Later, as you become more involved in projects, your focus naturally expands. You spend more time understanding business problems, collaborating with others, making design decisions, reviewing trade-offs, and thinking about systems at a higher level. The work becomes less about typing code and more about making good decisions.

## What AI is changing

AI is changing the balance between those two worlds.

It is making the programming part faster. It can generate boilerplate, propose implementations, explain unfamiliar code, draft tests, and help explore solutions quickly. That is a real productivity shift, and it is already visible in everyday work.

But the engineering part is still where judgment matters most.

AI can help produce code, but it does not truly own the problem. It does not carry responsibility for the long-term maintainability of a system. It does not understand business context the way a team does. It does not feel the pain of a bad design decision six months later when the code becomes harder to change.

So while AI reduces the cost of producing code, it increases the importance of knowing what code should be produced, why it should exist, and how it should fit into a larger system.

## Conclusion: acceleration does not remove accountability

To conclude, I want to highlight one thing clearly: AI is a powerful accelerator for producing code, but we are still responsible for what we ship.

Software does not live in a safe demo environment. It runs in production. It handles client data. It reaches sensitive environments such as embedded systems, airplanes, modern cars, and medical devices.

That is why we cannot delegate everything. We are still at the wheel.

As software engineers, we are accountable for the code we write, the code we review, and the code we deploy. This is the professionalism part of the job.

When incidents happen, we cannot hide behind AI. The responsibility remains ours.

So yes, software development has changed. And that is a great opportunity: AI can help us spend less time on repetitive coding and more time on real engineering.

But it is not a blanket permission to push unvalidated code.

Stay safe out there.
